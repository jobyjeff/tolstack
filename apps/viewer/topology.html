How to read the rails

  • A dot is an interface, a bar is the dimension between two of them. The grid beside the diagram holds one row per dimension, in the same walk order, grouped into components: the merged left cell spans each part's own rows.
  • A leader line is a part boundary. Each jogged line runs from an interface's dot to the seam between the two grid rows it separates — and it is only drawn where the dimensions meeting at that interface belong to different parts (or a clearance). An interface inside one part (several tolerances on one feature) gets no leader, which is what makes component membership visible at a glance.
  • A column is a branch, not a part. The walk is depth-first from the document's first node; a branch keeps its column until it rejoins or ends, which is why a fork fans out sideways and a grounded loop closes with a dashed curve back up to the interface it lands on. The walk's own main line runs in the rightmost column, hard against the grid, and each fork opens a new column to its left.
  • A bar's colour is its citation's confidence — the same green / amber / UNTRACED / NO CITATION vocabulary the elements table uses. The rails themselves are neutral on purpose: those four colours are reserved for provenance here, so lane identity is carried by position and by the part named on the row.
  • A dashed bar is a gap — its two interfaces share no part, so it crosses between lanes and has no rail of its own. A DERIVED gap carries no value at all: it is the quantity a study computes, and a study that puts it in its selection is refused.
  • Bar lengths are indicative, never measured. The toolbar's length setting draws every bar the same (uniform, the default), or stretches each in proportion to its tolerance band or its nominal size. In the scaled modes every bar keeps a minimum clickable length, and one drawn at that floor wears a small break mark across it: read the break as "too small — or unstated — to show to scale", never as a measured proportion. Whatever the setting, the diagram is scaled to fit the window — and where even the minimum lengths will not fit, every bar sits at that minimum and the page scrolls rather than shrinking a bar out of reach. The grid's rows stay evenly spaced through all of it: the diagram and the grid are centred against each other and the leader lines absorb the difference.
  • An accent-ringed dot marks a branch point. A study reaching one with two unconsumed selected edges raises BranchAmbiguity and names both. Which parallel path binds is a mechanics question this tool does not answer and must not learn to — see docs/DAG_TOPOLOGY.md, "Not a solver".
  • Every study says whether it passes, and by how much. The badge on the nav rail and at the head of the totals is the verdict; the margin beside it is how far the worst case is from the criterion, signed. A study nobody has yet written a criterion for says exactly that rather than staying blank. Where a badge is outlined in amber, a dimension the joint needs is missing from the chain — the verdict is then true of the model and not of the hardware, and what is missing is named underneath it.
  • A loud badge is a number you should not trust yet. unverified means nothing readable stands behind it; no tolerance recorded means the value is sourced but its plus/minus is not, so every spread it feeds is a lower bound. Badges appear on the row that earned them and again on the study that sums it. What's missing, under the totals, is the same story for the whole assembly, with what would close each gap.
  • Selecting a study highlights its chain, numbers each row with its place in the sum, prints each edge's own signed and scaled contribution, and puts the totals in the footer below. Every one of those numbers came out of tolerance_stack.topology.summarize() → fold(). Nothing on this page is added up in JavaScript.
